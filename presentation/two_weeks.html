01/ 10 HCM crossbridge modeling

The big picture · why this matters

One tiny muscle model → a whole-heart simulation

The problem

In HCM the heart squeezes too hard and relaxes badly. Doctors can see the thick wall, but not the broken molecule causing it.

My job

Find the crossbridge-level setting that drives the extra force, then hand that number to Julia's whole-heart geometry model as an input.

The method

Compare models of increasing detail (3, 4, and 6 "states") and let the computer fit each one to real control vs HCM heartbeat data.

A "state" = one pose a muscle motor can be in (resting, primed, gripping, pulling). More states = more realistic, but more knobs to tune.

July 7 · sensitivity analysis

Which knobs actually change the force?

I nudged every one of ~30 settings by 10% and measured how much peak force moved. Bars to the right = raises force, left = lowers it.

  • Big movers: how sticky the calcium switch is (k_on / k_off) and the muscle's resting length.
  • Middle: attachment rate k_3, motor density, and the SRX release rate k_1.
  • Near zero: most of the new 6-state coupling knobs — they shape the timing, not the peak height.

The knob that looked useless actually controls relaxation

Left graph — peak force

Barely changes, then falls off a cliff. That flat top is why it looked "useless" in the bar chart.

Right graph — relaxation time

Climbs steadily: crank this knob and the muscle takes much longer to let go. It's a relaxation knob, not a force knob.

July 14 & 20 · fixing the physics

Three foundation bugs that were quietly wrong

01

Titin was pushing when it should rest

The elastic "rubber band" protein was set to resist squishing as hard as stretching, producing impossible negative force. Real titin only pulls when stretched. Fixed → force can't go negative anymore.

02

Wrong resting length

The model sat at 1265 nm; the lab works near 1000 nm. Re-based everything to 1000 nm so simulated and real muscle start from the same place.

03

The "snap-back" release was missing

Added a sharp let-go step when a motor is pulled too far. This single fix took the 4-state disease error from 0.37 → 0.03 — a 10× jump in realism.

A hidden pipeline file was also missing since a June cleanup — no fit could actually re-run until I restored it.

July 23 · timing check

Is the model's force arriving at the right moment?

The lowest error sits at −16 ms (control) and −12 ms (HCM) — meaning the model's force currently rises about a hair too early. A ~14 ms tweak to the calcium-onset timing would tighten it further. Small, but worth logging.

08/ 10 HCM crossbridge modeling

The honest catch

The winning model can't yet name one culprit

What I hoped

"HCM is driven by this one knob turned up this much" — a single number to hand Julia.

What I found

Restarting the fit from different guesses lands on different knob combinations that all fit almost equally well. k_1 ranged 3–17×; several knobs trade off against each other.

Why: the 6-state model has more knobs than the single heartbeat can pin down — like having two unknowns and one equation. This is the real headline: I can't responsibly report a single HCM driver yet.

Where this goes next

Takeaways & the plan

What's solid

  • 6-state model fits both conditions best (error < 0.006).
  • Physics foundation is now correct (titin, length, snap-back).
  • Every knob's role is mapped; k_7_1 = relaxation control.

Next steps

  • Run many restarts on a computing cluster to test identifiability.
  • Add constraints / more data to narrow the HCM driver.
  • Only then hand a defensible number to Julia's heart model.

Bottom line: the model is finally trustworthy, the physics is right, and I know precisely what question to attack next.
